Research

My research in recent years focuses on probing the nature of dark matter dark matter q&a Q: what is dark matter? A: short answer — we don’t know. It’s a hypothetical form of matter physicists invoke to explain observations that are otherwise very hard to explain: galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing, the CMB, the Bullet Cluster, and large-scale structure. It “should” not interact electromagnetically (hence invisible, or “dark”), and we have no direct evidence of it interacting with ordinary matter. Q: then why do we need it? A: because it explains a wide range of observations. Even if it’s “just” a model, it’s a good, useful model — and even if dark matter isn’t literally “matter”, something equivalent must exist to explain those observations. Physics is all about models. “All models are wrong. Some are useful.” — deploying computer simulations to predict how DM is distributed in the Universe, distributions that can leave observational imprints. Specifically, I work mainly on a group of DM models — self-interacting dark matter (SIDM sidm q&a Q: what is SIDM? A: whereas CDM is collisionless and only interacts gravitationally, SIDM adds particle-physics scatterings, characterized by their strength (the cross section), plus extra degrees of freedom — some models include energy dissipation or transitions between excitation states during a scattering event. Q: why does SIDM feel like just adding a degree of freedom to CDM, so physicists can fine-tune and force-fit the observations? It sounds like an ugly manipulation! A: you’re not wrong. But I’d frame it differently: assuming DM is completely collisionless is actually the stronger assumption — every form of ordinary matter interacts (that’s how we observe it). It’s more natural to put some placeholder self-interaction in there, and then try to constrain its strength. ), a hot candidate in recent years for resolving a series of small-scale observational anomalies not easily accommodated by standard cold dark matter (CDM). In particular, I mostly use idealized/controlled/isolated/staged simulationsI.e., not a full cosmological simulation box, but one or a few main halos and their subhalos. The unique advantage is good/scalable resolution at reasonable computational cost, together with clean physics (i.e., you can separate the physics you want to study from complicated environmental effects) — at the cost of “idealized assumptions” (hopefully not too significant at the present stage), such as spherical symmetry in initializing halos, and isolation from the cosmic environment (yes, this is a double-edged sword). to study the evolution of SIDM (sub)halos, in the context of SIDM core-formation to core-collapse.

Core-collapse, evaporation and tidal effects: the life story of a self-interacting dark matter subhalo

Z. C. Zeng, A. Peter, X. Du, et al. · MNRAS, 2022 · arXiv:2110.00259

The starting point of the thread: how one SIDM subhalo evolves under the combined action of gravothermal core collapse, evaporation against the host halo, and tidal effects — and whether, or never, it core-collapses.

  • Built a hybrid semi-analytic + N-body framework that speeds up SIDM subhalo simulations in the core-collapse regime — the host is modeled as an analytic SIDM distribution, including its evaporation effect on the subhalo.
  • Coupling to the host can accelerate, delay or even disrupt core collapse: evaporation and tidal heating act as heating terms that counter it, while internal heat outflow is a cooling term that drives it. fig. 1
  • The tidal field alone plays a complex role — sometimes accelerating core collapse, sometimes decelerating it — because tidal heating and tidal stripping work against each other. fig. 2
  • Derived the critical (minimum) concentration for core collapse within a Hubble time for isolated halos vs subhalos. For a constant cross section it is 2–3× higher than typical cosmological values — constant-σ models are therefore very unlikely to produce core-collapsed substructure. fig. 3

Till the core collapses: the evolution and properties of self-interacting dark matter subhalos

Z. C. Zeng, A. Peter, X. Du, et al. · Phys. Rev. D, 2025 · arXiv:2310.09910

From one subhalo to a realistic population — how often, and on what mass scales, does core collapse actually happen?

  • Built a “hierarchical” simulation framework that follows a realistic population of SIDM subhalos drawn from merger trees — subhalos are grouped into smaller sub-simulations by their mass at infall, each with hierarchical particle resolution.
  • At the population level, a significant fraction (>10%) of subhalos can core-collapse with certain velocity-dependent SIDM cross sections. fig. 1
  • A typical velocity-dependent cross section (violet line in fig. 2) that drives substantial core collapse also broadens the diversity of subhalo inner masses — not shifting all halos to the compact side, but diversifying them.
  • The two velocity-dependent models with significant core collapse match recent HST substructure-lensing inferences of ultra-high subhalo central slopes. fig. 3
  • For substructure lensing: the 1-point function is only sensitive to core collapse in massive subhalos — the column density of small, compact subhalos is outweighed by large, diffuse ones — while the 2-point correlation function can distinguish core collapse at different halo-mass scales.

Diversity and universality: evolution of dwarf galaxies with self-interacting dark matter

Z. C. Zeng, A. Peter, X. Du, et al. · Phys. Rev. D, 2025 · arXiv:2412.14621

Full data behind fig. 1 — every simulated dwarf satellite, all SIDM models and orbits →

Populate the (sub)halo with stellar particles, trace how the stars respond to SIDM physics and host–subhalo interactions, and build “tidal tracks” that anchor stellar evolution to dark-matter-only metrics.

  • SIDM satellites do follow a common pattern — “tidal tracks” — using the inner dark-matter mass as the anchor of their evolution. fig. 1
  • The actual diversity of SIDM satellites’ stellar components is much larger than in CDM — especially in the core-collapse context — confirming, for the first time, the diversity predicted from dark-matter-only simulations, now with real stars.
  • In the stellar-size–stellar-mass plane, the diffuse side of dwarfs is easily matched, but ultra-compact dwarfs (UCDs) in clusters remain about an order of magnitude more compact — a qualitative comparison only, since our simulations sit at the galaxy-group scale, not the cluster scale where UCDs live. fig. 2

Feeding the Void: co-evolution of the SIDM-seeded black hole and the dark halo after core-collapse

Z. C. Zeng, A. Peter, L. Strigari, et al. · 2026 · arXiv:2608.xxxxx

What happens after SIDM core-collapse seeds a black hole? How does the black hole keep growing, and how does the host halo evolve in response?

  • Built an N-body framework (DM-only) that brackets post-collapse dark-matter accretion with two complementary schemes — multi-round core-collapse (lower bound) and continuous accretion (upper bound). fig. 1
  • The BH mass-growth trajectory flattens on a ∼2 Gyr timescale, reaching 5%–12% of the halo mass — extending beyond the ∼1% single-episode SMFP estimates. fig. 1
  • A comprehensive review and comparison with contemporary studies of SIDM-seeded black holes. fig. 2
  • Sustained accretion drives a “gravothermal supply chain”: the outer halo feeds the core, which feeds the black hole, severely depleting the inner density (≲2 rs).
  • The resulting halo profile qualitatively resembles a very recent lensing anomaly: Vegetti et al. 2026 report a ∼106 M⊙ perturber in JVAS B1938+666, whose 2D projected mass is best fit by an unresolved central point mass plus a shallow, extended disk. fig. 3

Bypassed core formation in Milky Way-mass SIDM halos: implications for the Local Group past-pericenter scenario

Z. C. Zeng, O. Hartl, L. Strigari, et al. · submitted to Phys. Rev. D, 2026 · arXiv:2604.08647

A study of Milky Way–mass systems, in isolation and within the Local Group — with a specific focus on a possible past pericenter.

  • Milky Way–mass galaxies can bypass the SIDM core-formation phase and enter core collapse directly — a behavior that appears independent of the cross section. fig. 1
  • This bypassing is sensitive to the stellar-halo-mass ratio: the higher the stellar mass fraction, the more likely it happens — and the Milky Way–mass scale is where the SMHM relation peaks. fig. 2
  • In the past-pericenter scenario, the disk/bulge is insensitive to pericenters above ∼10 kpc but sensitive to the SIDM cross section; the diffuse stellar halo is the opposite — insensitive to the SIDM model but sensitive to pericenters below ∼100 kpc. fig. 3
